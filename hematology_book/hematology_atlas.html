Part I — Morphology • Chapter 1

1. How to Read a Peripheral Blood Smear

The blood film is the oldest and still one of the most informative tests in hematology. A well-made, well-stained smear lets you assess all three cell lines at once.

Making & examining the film

  • Preparation: a small drop of EDTA blood is spread with a spreader slide at ~30–45°, air-dried, then stained with a Romanowsky stain (Wright or Wright–Giemsa).
  • The reading zone: examine where red cells just stop overlapping — the “monolayer”. Here central pallor and true morphology are visible.
  • Systematic scan: low power (10×) for distribution & clumps → high dry / oil (40–100×) for detailed morphology → estimate a differential count.
What to report Red cells: size, colour, shape, inclusions. White cells: numbers, differential, maturity, reactive changes. Platelets: number (estimate), size, clumping. Always correlate with the automated CBC.

Part I — Morphology • Chapter 2

2. Red Blood Cell Morphology

RBC abnormalities are described in four axes: size, colour, shape, and inclusions. A normal RBC is 7–8 µm, biconcave, with central pallor.

Size (anisocytosis)

  • Microcyte (<6 µm) — iron deficiency, thalassemia.
  • Macrocyte (>9 µm) — B12/folate deficiency, liver disease, alcohol.
  • Anisocytosis — variation in size (high RDW).

Colour (chromia)

  • Hypochromia — enlarged pallor; low hemoglobin.
  • Polychromasia — bluish young cells (reticulocytes); marrow response.

Shape (poikilocytosis)

  • Sickle cell — HbS polymerization.
  • Target cell — liver disease, thalassemia, HbC.
  • Spherocyte — hereditary spherocytosis, immune hemolysis.
  • Schistocyte — fragments; MAHA, DIC, mechanical valve.
  • Teardrop (dacrocyte) — marrow fibrosis.
  • Elliptocyte — hereditary elliptocytosis.

RBC inclusions (brief)

Basophilic stipplingAggregated ribosomesLead poisoning, thalassemia

3. White Blood Cell Morphology

Five mature leukocytes circulate. Distinguish them by nuclear shape, cytoplasmic colour, and granules.

CellApprox. %Key morphologyMain role
Neutrophil40–70%3–5 lobed nucleus, fine lilac granulesBacterial phagocytosis
Lymphocyte20–45%Round dense nucleus, thin blue rimAdaptive immunity (B/T)
Monocyte2–10%Large kidney-shaped nucleus, grey-blue cytoplasm, vacuolesMacrophage precursor, antigen presentation
Eosinophil1–6%Bilobed nucleus, bright red-orange granulesParasites, allergy
Basophil<1%Dark blue-black granules obscuring nucleusHistamine, hypersensitivity
Left shift Increase in immature neutrophils (band forms, metamyelocytes). Suggests acute infection or marrow stress.
Toxic changes Toxic granulation, Döhle bodies, and vacuolation indicate severe infection/sepsis.

Reactive vs. neoplastic clues

  • Reactive lymphocytes (e.g. EBV) — large, abundant pale-blue cytoplasm hugging surrounding red cells.
  • Blasts — high nucleus:cytoplasm ratio, fine chromatin, nucleoli; Auer rods suggest AML. Always flag for review.

4. Platelet Morphology

Platelets are anucleate cytoplasmic fragments of megakaryocytes, 2–4 µm, with fine purple granules. Normal count 150–450 ×10⁹/L.

On the film

  • Number: roughly 8–20 platelets per oil field ≈ normal; each platelet ≈ 15–20 ×10⁹/L.
  • Large / giant platelets: ITP, myeloproliferative disease, inherited macrothrombocytopenias.
  • Platelet clumps: EDTA-induced pseudothrombocytopenia — recollect in citrate.
  • Platelet satellitism: platelets rosetting around neutrophils (EDTA artefact).
Always confirm low counts A low automated platelet count must be checked against the film before reporting. Clumps, giant platelets, or fibrin strands can falsely lower the analyser count.
Critical value Marked thrombocytopenia (<20 ×10⁹/L) carries a spontaneous bleeding risk and is usually a call-back result.

From morphology to function

Platelet number and size on the smear are the visual bridge to the next part of this atlas: platelets are the first responders of hemostasis. We now follow them into clot formation.

5. Overview of Hemostasis

Hemostasis is the balanced process that stops bleeding at a site of injury while keeping blood fluid everywhere else. It has three overlapping phases.

PhaseMain playersResult
1. Primary hemostasisPlatelets, von Willebrand factor, vessel wallUnstable platelet plug (seconds–minutes)
2. Secondary hemostasisCoagulation factors → thrombin → fibrinStable fibrin-reinforced clot
3. FibrinolysisPlasmin, tPAControlled clot breakdown & remodelling
The balance concept Procoagulant forces (platelets, clotting factors) are constantly opposed by anticoagulant and fibrinolytic forces (antithrombin, protein C/S, plasmin). Bleeding or thrombosis results when this balance is disturbed.

Part II — Coagulation • Chapter 6

6. Primary Hemostasis — The Platelet Plug

Within seconds of vessel injury, platelets form the first seal in three steps: adhesion → activation → aggregation.

The three steps

  • Adhesion: exposed subendothelial collagen binds von Willebrand factor (vWF), which tethers platelets via receptor GPIb.
  • Activation: platelets change shape, extend pseudopods, and release granule contents (ADP, thromboxane A₂, serotonin), recruiting more platelets.
  • Aggregation: activated GPIIb/IIIa receptors bind fibrinogen, cross-linking platelets into a plug.
Disorders of primary hemostasis Present with mucocutaneous bleeding: petechiae, easy bruising, epistaxis, menorrhagia. Examples: thrombocytopenia, von Willebrand disease, aspirin/clopidogrel effect, Glanzmann (GPIIb/IIIa), Bernard–Soulier (GPIb).
Test link Screened with platelet count and platelet function assays (PFA-100/closure time); vWF activity for suspected VWD.

7. Secondary Hemostasis — The Coagulation Cascade

A chain of serine-protease reactions converts fibrinogen to fibrin. The classic model has an intrinsic and an extrinsic arm meeting in a common pathway. This model maps directly onto the lab tests.

Fig 7.1 — Coagulation cascade. Factor numbering in Roman numerals; “a” = activated form. Modern cell-based models emphasise that tissue factor/VIIa initiates coagulation in vivo, with the intrinsic arm amplifying thrombin — but the classic diagram still explains PT and aPTT.

Vitamin K–dependent factors II, VII, IX, X (and proteins C & S). Target of warfarin. Factor VII has the shortest half-life → PT rises first.
Cofactors & calcium Factors V and VIII are cofactors (not enzymes). Ca²⁺ (factor IV) and phospholipid surfaces are required throughout.

8. Anticoagulants & Fibrinolysis

Clotting must be confined to the injury site. Natural inhibitors localise the clot, and fibrinolysis removes it once healing begins.

Natural anticoagulant systems

SystemActionNote
Antithrombin (AT)Inhibits thrombin & Xa (IXa, XIa)Accelerated by heparin
Protein C + Protein SInactivate factors Va & VIIIaActivated by thrombin–thrombomodulin; vitamin K–dependent
Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI)Inhibits TF–VIIa & XaDown-regulates initiation
Factor V Leiden A mutation making factor Va resistant to protein C (“activated protein C resistance”) — the commonest inherited thrombophilia.

Fibrinolysis

  • Plasminogen → plasmin, driven by tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and urokinase.
  • Plasmin digests fibrin, releasing fibrin degradation products (FDPs) and D-dimer.
  • Regulated by PAI-1 (inhibits tPA) and α2-antiplasmin (inhibits plasmin).
  • D-dimer is a specific product of cross-linked fibrin breakdown — raised in DVT/PE, DIC, and many acute states.

Reading the pattern

  • PT ↑, aPTT normal → extrinsic (factor VII), early warfarin.
  • aPTT ↑, PT normal → intrinsic (VIII, IX, XI), heparin, lupus anticoagulant.
  • Both ↑ → common pathway, liver disease, DIC, high-dose anticoagulation.
  • Mixing study: corrects → factor deficiency; fails to correct → inhibitor (e.g. lupus anticoagulant).
DIC pattern ↑PT, ↑aPTT, ↓platelets, ↓fibrinogen, ↑↑D-dimer, plus schistocytes on the film — a consumptive coagulopathy.

Part IV — Beginner's Guide • Chapter 13

13. Step 1 — Making a Good Blood Film

Everything starts with a good film. A bad smear cannot be rescued — if it is uneven, too thick, or too short, make a new one before you look.

The wedge (push) technique

  1. Place a small drop (~2–3 mm) of well-mixed EDTA blood near one end of a clean slide.
  2. Bring a spreader slide back into the drop at a 30–45° angle and let the blood spread along its edge.
  3. Push forward smoothly and quickly in one motion. Do not press down.
  4. Air-dry immediately (wave, do not blow), then stain with Wright / Wright–Giemsa.
Angle rule of thumb Blood too thick (high Hct) → use a smaller angle / faster push. Blood too thin (anemia) → use a larger angle / slower push.
Where you will read The monolayer, just before the feathered edge, is the only place morphology is reliable. The head is too thick; the very edge distorts the cells.

Part IV — Beginner's Guide • Chapter 14

14. Step 2 — Microscope Set-up & Scanning

Move from low to high power, and scan in a fixed pattern so you never count the same cell twice or miss an area.

Magnification ladder

  • 10× (low power): overall quality, cell distribution, clumps, and to find the monolayer. Estimate WBC numbers.
  • 40× (high dry): WBC differential and general red cell morphology.
  • 100× (oil immersion): fine detail — inclusions, parasites, platelet morphology. Add a drop of oil directly on the film.

The battlement scan

In the monolayer, move the slide in a battlement (crenellation) track — up, across, down, across — so every field is new. This gives a representative differential.

Counting tip Count 100 white cells in this track for a manual differential, keeping a tally of each type.

15. Step 3 — A Beginner's Read, Step by Step

Always read in the same order. A fixed routine means you never forget a cell line.

#StepPowerWhat you are asking
1Check smear quality & staining10×Is it even? Well stained? Find the monolayer.
2Scan cell distribution10×Any clumps, rouleaux, or large abnormal cells?
3Estimate WBC number10×Roughly increased, normal, or decreased?
4Red cell morphology40×Size, colour, shape, inclusions (vs Part II).
5WBC differential (100 cells)40–100×Proportion of each leukocyte; any left shift?
6Look for abnormal WBCs100×Blasts, Auer rods, atypical lymphocytes (Part III).
7Estimate platelets100×Number, size, clumps (× ~15–20 per oil field).
8Correlate & report—Does it match the analyser CBC? Flag discrepancies.
Beginner pitfalls Reading in the thick head; using oil before finding the monolayer; mistaking stain precipitate or platelets on top of red cells for inclusions; reporting a low platelet count without checking for clumps.
Golden habit Same order, every time: quality → distribution → red cells → white cells → platelets → correlate.

17. The Anemia Approach by MCV

When Hb is low, the fastest first step is to split anemia by MCV, then add the reticulocyte count to see whether the marrow is responding.

Fig 17.1 — First-pass classification of anemia. Confirm the suspected cause on the film (e.g. microcytic + hypochromic → iron deficiency; macro-ovalocytes + hypersegmented neutrophils → B12/folate).

Reticulocytes decide "why" In normocytic anemia especially, a high reticulocyte count points to loss/destruction (bleeding, hemolysis), while a low count points to reduced production (marrow, chronic disease, renal failure).

19. Blood Parasites — Malaria on the Film

The blood film is still the reference method for diagnosing malaria. Examine a well-stained film carefully at oil immersion — parasites live inside the red cells.

How the lab looks

  • Thick film: concentrates parasites — best to detect infection.
  • Thin film: preserves red cell detail — best to speciate and estimate parasitemia.
  • Report the species and the % parasitemia (parasitized red cells per 100).
Why species matters P. falciparum can cause high parasitemia and severe/cerebral malaria — high parasitemia or falciparum is a medical emergency. A single negative film does not exclude malaria; repeat over 48–72 h if suspicion is high.

Part VI — Blood Cancers • Chapter 20

20. Overview & Classification of Blood Cancers

Hematological malignancies are clonal cancers of blood-forming cells. Two questions organise them all: which lineage? (myeloid vs lymphoid) and how fast? (acute vs chronic).

Fig 20.1 — The lineage × tempo grid. Acute = immature blasts that fail to mature (rapid, aggressive); chronic = accumulation of more mature cells (slower).

Leukemia vs lymphoma vs myeloma Leukemia — primarily in blood/marrow. Lymphoma — solid masses in lymph nodes/tissue. Myeloma — malignant plasma cells in marrow making one (monoclonal) antibody.
Diagnosis is multimodal Morphology raises the suspicion; definitive diagnosis needs flow cytometry (immunophenotype), cytogenetics/molecular (e.g. BCR-ABL1), and often marrow biopsy.

21. Acute Leukemias — AML & ALL

Acute leukemias are defined by ≥20% blasts in blood or marrow. They present rapidly with the consequences of marrow failure: anemia, infection (neutropenia) and bleeding (thrombocytopenia).

AML — mostly adults; may show gum infiltration; APL subtype is a coagulation emergency (DIC).

ALL — commonest childhood cancer; may involve CNS and (in boys) testes.

FeatureAMLALL
Typical ageAdultsChildren (peak 2–5 y)
Blast clueAuer rods, granulesNo Auer rods, scant cytoplasm
Lineage markersMPO+, CD13/CD33TdT+, CD19/CD10 (B) or CD3 (T)
Emergency Suspected acute leukemia (many blasts, pancytopenia, or blasts with Auer rods) must be flagged and referred the same day — some subtypes deteriorate within hours.

22. Chronic Leukemias — CML & CLL

Chronic leukemias accumulate relatively mature cells, so they are often found on a routine CBC in a well-looking patient. The two big ones are myeloid (CML) and lymphoid (CLL).

CML — driven by BCR-ABL1 (Philadelphia chromosome); treated with tyrosine-kinase inhibitors. Can transform to a blast crisis.

CLL — commonest adult leukemia in the West; elderly; often asymptomatic lymphocytosis ± lymphadenopathy.

Key contrast CML = too many maturing myeloid cells across all stages; CLL = too many identical mature lymphocytes. Confirm CML with BCR-ABL1 and CLL with flow cytometry.

24. Myeloproliferative & Myelodysplastic Neoplasms

Two related myeloid groups: MPN — the marrow makes too many mature cells; MDS — the marrow makes dysplastic, ineffective cells (cytopenias despite a busy marrow).

NeoplasmOverproduced cellClue
